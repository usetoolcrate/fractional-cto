Still on WordPress?

It runs roughly two out of five websites on earth — which is exactly why automated attacks hammer it around the clock. The site only stays safe as long as somebody keeps feeding it: core updates, theme updates, plugin updates, and every update can quietly break something else. That somebody is currently you.

What changes: plain, dependency-free code — nobody on your team patches a plugin again.

Still on "a developer built it years ago"?

And now nobody answers the phone, nobody has the passwords, and everyone's afraid to touch it. That's exactly what door one — "take over my site" — exists for.

What changes: one point of contact who documents everything, so the next person isn't guessing either.

Stuck in an agency queue?

Every change goes through a ticket, a project manager, and a two-week wait — and by the time it ships, the reason you needed it is gone. That's not a website problem. It's an accountability problem.

What changes: one accountable owner, not a queue — a fixed price and a real deadline, in writing.

The questions everyone asks

What if we want to edit content ourselves?

In all my years of retainer work, clients have asked me to make the change every single time — that's why they keep me around. Messaging your web guy is faster than fighting a page editor.

But if you have genuinely high-frequency content — a jobs board, a news feed, an events calendar — I build that one section as a simple editor made for exactly your content. The rest of the site stays bulletproof.

What happens if something happens to you?

Everything lives in a plain, documented repository that you own. Any competent developer can take over tomorrow morning. That's a stronger continuity story than most business websites have today, where the site is trapped inside software a shrinking number of people understand.

Do you take ownership of our website or accounts?

No. Your business keeps control of its domain, accounts, data, and website. My job is to take responsibility for the technical work we agree on, not to take possession of your assets.

When a handoff makes sense, the work is documented so another qualified person can understand it.

Why is there no hourly rate?

Because hours measure my time, not your outcome. You're buying "the site ships on this date" and "changes go live the same day" — so that's what the contract says, and that's what the invoice reads. If I get faster at my job, you shouldn't have to renegotiate; you should just get faster service.

How fast do changes really go live?

The contract guarantees acknowledgment within 2 business hours and most content changes live the same business day. In practice, small changes often ship within the hour. I promise the number I can keep on my worst day and beat it on every other one.

Can you really take over a site you didn't build?

Yes — that's what the stabilization month is for. I get your site into version control, document how it works, and make it safe to change before promising same-day turnaround. If what I find means a rebuild would genuinely cost you less than maintaining what you have, I'll show you that math instead of quietly billing you for fighting it.

What does a rebuild not include?

The scope is a one-page document we both sign before kickoff: which pages, which integrations, what gets migrated. Anything outside it goes on the next month's outcome list instead of silently inflating the project. That discipline is why I can put a real price and a real date on delivery.

Why I work this way

I've been building websites and web applications for over fifteen years. For most of those years I billed like everyone else: hourly, with reports. And I watched the same thing happen on every project — the moment hours enter the conversation, trust leaves it. The client starts wondering what they're paying for. The developer starts performing busyness. Everybody loses.

So I stopped. Now I sell two things: a finished site by a date we both signed, and the promise that when you need something changed, it's done the same day. My clients tend to stay for years — I think that's why.

I keep my client list short on purpose. There's no account manager between you and me, no junior developer doing the work while I take the calls. When you message, I answer.
